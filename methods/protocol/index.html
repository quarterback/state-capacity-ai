Method II

The Protocol

We're building AI systems that make consequential decisions about people's lives, and we have no shared infrastructure for making those decisions legible, bounded, or accountable. The Judgment Router is that infrastructure.

The Problem of Delegation

Agentic systems are designed to complete tasks. Their value proposition is autonomy—give them a goal, and they figure out the steps. This works until the steps involve consequences the agent cannot evaluate: legal liability, ethical judgment, political sensitivity, or irreversible harm.

The standard response is "human-in-the-loop." But most human-in-the-loop implementations are theater. The human receives a notification, sees a summary the agent wrote about its own work, and clicks approve. There is no genuine decision point because the human lacks the context to disagree.

The 90/10 problem

Routine decisions—the 90%—work fine with automation. But the remaining 10% carry outsized institutional risk. These are the edge cases, the novel situations, the judgment calls that define whether an institution is trustworthy. Without infrastructure to identify and route these decisions differently, the 10% gets processed with the same automation as the 90%.

The authority problem

In paper workflows, decision authority was traceable. Jan reviewed the application, escalated to Bill, who got sign-off from Steve. In agentic systems, this chain vanishes. The model made a recommendation, the system acted on it, and nobody can reconstruct who authorized what.

The Method of Capacity

The Judgment Router is middleware that sits between agent recommendations and action execution. It evaluates every proposed action against four signals before deciding how to proceed.

The four signals

  • Uncertainty: Data ambiguity or conflicting requirements. When the inputs are unclear, the system slows down rather than guessing.
  • Stakes: Fiscal impact, downstream risk, stakeholder scope. A $50 decision and a $50,000 decision do not get the same treatment.
  • Authority: Required sign-off level vs. current delegation. The system knows what it's allowed to do and what requires human authorization.
  • Novelty: Familiar patterns vs. unprecedented scenarios. When a situation falls outside known parameters, the system flags it rather than extrapolating.

Routing paths

Based on signal evaluation, actions route through one of four paths:

  • Fast Path: Immediate execution. Low uncertainty, low stakes, within authority, familiar pattern.
  • Slow Path: Verification needed. Elevated on one or more signals but within recoverable bounds.
  • Human Escalation: High-stakes or authority-exceeding. The action is blocked until a human with appropriate authority reviews and approves.
  • Specialist Referral: Domain mismatch. The system recognizes it is not the right decision-maker and routes to someone who is.

Three core artifacts

The framework defines three machine-readable artifacts that make delegated authority explicit:

  • Strategic Context Documents: Human-readable YAML defining organizational priorities, constraints, authority levels, financial limits, communication limits, and escalation contacts.
  • Authority Envelopes: Machine-readable JSON containers that agents carry during execution. Scoped to specific tasks with granted authorities, time limits, and mandatory escalation triggers.
  • Decision Receipts: Structured audit trails documenting what action was proposed, how it was evaluated against authority boundaries and priority weights, and whether it was approved, escalated, or blocked.

Refusal as a feature

Systems that never say "I can't" are systems that have no limits. The Protocol treats refusal as a core capability, not a failure state. An agent that refuses an action it shouldn't take is working correctly. An agent that proceeds when it shouldn't is the one that's broken.
