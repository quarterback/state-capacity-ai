Five Principles of Context Engineering Design

Context engineering encompasses the full environment AI models enter—the systems, assumptions, and consequences they create.

Context engineering typically describes managing context states for LLMs—retrieval, memory architectures, prompt optimization. This guide expands the frame to include deployment environments, embedded assumptions, and consequences for the people affected.

Principle 01

Context as Environment

Where is this system deployed? What assumptions does it carry? What power does it hold? Context extends beyond the token window into the full environment the system enters.

What this means

Traditional context engineering treats context as input: documents to retrieve, conversation history to maintain, data to compress. Context also includes the institutional setting, power dynamics, and stakes of getting it wrong.

Why it matters

An AI system in a benefits eligibility process carries different context than the same system in a customer service chatbot. The environment shapes what "success" means, who absorbs failure, and which guardrails are needed.

In practice

  • Document the deployment context: where, for whom, with what alternatives
  • Map the power dynamics: who can exit vs. who is captive to the system
  • Identify environmental constraints: regulatory requirements, due process needs
  • Design for the actual context, including its messiest edges

Principle 02

Disintermediation Analysis

Evaluate whether AI mediation strengthens or weakens direct relationships, capabilities, and decision-making.

What this means

Disintermediation occurs when AI interfaces interpret and act on behalf of people, separating them from direct relationships, capabilities, and decisions.

In practice

  • Identify which capabilities people lose access to
  • Map where human judgment is being removed from processes
  • Document when AI mediation creates new barriers
  • Preserve escalation paths to human decision-makers
Case Study: Customer Service Chatbots

When chatbots become the only resolution channel, users lose: the ability to explain nuanced problems, access to empathy and judgment, the option to escalate immediately, and the relationship with the company.

Principle 03

Context Legibility (Auditability)

Making AI reasoning visible for oversight, advocacy, and the critical moments when someone needs to understand what went wrong.

The nutrition label argument

Most people skip nutrition labels. They still matter because regulators can audit them, researchers can analyze patterns, advocates can identify problems, and they exist for the rare case when someone truly needs to know.

Who needs legibility

  • Oversight bodies: Auditors and regulators verifying systems work as intended
  • Advocates: Organizations representing affected communities who need evidence
  • Researchers: People documenting patterns and problems
  • Staff: Caseworkers who must explain outcomes to constituents
  • Users in crisis: The person whose benefits were denied who needs to understand why

In practice

  • Build audit interfaces, even ones hidden by default
  • Document what data the system accesses and its sources
  • Make assumptions explicit and correctable
  • Provide step-by-step reasoning for decisions
  • Give users controls to see, edit, and delete what systems know

Principle 04

Escalation & Refusal Design

Systems must recognize their limits, refuse tasks beyond their competence, and escalate to human judgment when needed.

What this means

AI systems need the ability to refuse tasks beyond their competence, escalate to human judgment, and admit uncertainty rather than simulate confidence.

In practice

  • Design explicit "I don't know" responses with clear next steps
  • Build clear escalation paths to human decision-makers
  • Set confidence thresholds that trigger refusal or escalation
  • Make refusal graceful: explain why and what to do instead
  • Track refusal and escalation patterns to identify system limits

Principle 05

Capability Preservation

Measure whether people gain or lose skills when AI mediates their tasks.

What this means

When AI mediates tasks, people can lose underlying knowledge and skills. The system may work efficiently today yet leave people dependent and unable to function when it fails.

In practice

  • Document capabilities people had before AI mediation
  • Design for graceful degradation: systems that revert to human operation
  • Help people understand the reasoning behind outcomes
  • Preserve parallel paths so AI is never the only way to accomplish tasks
  • Monitor for skill atrophy in staff who rely on AI tools
Example: Navigation Apps

GPS navigation is efficient, yet many people have lost the ability to read maps or navigate by landmarks. When the system fails—no signal, dead battery—they are stranded. Public infrastructure should support both AI efficiency and human capability.

Background

This framework extends Agent Experience (AX) principles for deployment contexts where failure carries real consequences: government services, healthcare, customer support, HR.
