AI Disclosure Standards

A practical framework for making algorithmic authority visible in public services. Templates, classification tools, and worked examples that agencies can deploy under real-world constraints.

The Disclosure Gap

The Algorithm Sees You. You Should See the Algorithm.

When banks use algorithms to deny credit, federal law requires disclosure. The Fair Credit Reporting Act mandates specific reasons for denial, a copy of the report used, contact information to dispute errors, and your rights explained in plain language. This applies even when AI makes the decision. Companies cannot hide behind "the algorithm is too complex to explain."

When government uses algorithms to make decisions about people's lives, no equivalent requirement exists.

Where AI Operates Without Disclosure

AI is already being used across public services with no disclosure that AI was involved, no explanation of what data was used, no clear path to challenge algorithmic errors, and no accountability when the system is wrong.

Why This Matters Now

Compliance pressure is forcing rapid AI deployment. HR1 requires states to cut Medicaid error rates or face federal funding clawbacks. States are turning to AI to meet impossible timelines.

  • The risk: Agencies deploy untested vendor systems. Caseworkers defend decisions they don't understand. People lose benefits due to unidentified errors.
  • The gap: Private sector AI tools are imported into public services without public accountability infrastructure.
  • What's missing: A lightweight standard for disclosure—something agencies can actually implement under pressure. Not aspirational principles. Not academic frameworks. Just: here's the template, fill it out, publish it, make authority visible.

Three Tools to Close the Gap

Tool 1: AI Enablement Statement

A public-facing disclosure functioning like a nutrition label for algorithmic authority. It tells people what the AI does, where humans are in the loop, how to challenge a decision, and how the system is monitored for errors.

A one-page notice that shows where automation sits in a service, what humans control, and how people can challenge outcomes.

Template Fields

Every AI Enablement Statement should include these sections:

  1. Service — Name of the service
  2. Agency — Responsible agency or department
  3. Purpose and context — Why this system exists now
  4. AI role — What the system does and when
  5. Human role — Who decides and why
  6. Contestability — How a person asks for review
  7. Oversight — Audits, cadence, triggers
  8. Limitations — What the system cannot do
  9. Data sources — What feeds the system
  10. Contact — Where to report errors

It gives people a clear view of how decisions are made. It protects workers by stating boundaries. It forces teams to plan oversight before launch.

Tool 2: Decision-Grade AI Taxonomy

A classification system for AI authority levels. Knowing where a system sits on this scale determines the strength of disclosure and oversight required.

Level Name Description Examples
1 Advisory AI suggests or highlights information. Human decides everything. Risk scores, checklists, flagging
2 Assistive AI completes routine decisions when all criteria are met. Human handles complexity and exceptions. Auto-approvals, renewals
3 Determinative AI makes binding decisions. Human reviews only on appeal. High risk. Mandatory investigations, automated denials

Systems that cross from assistive to determinative require additional public disclosure, audit mechanisms, and human appeal paths. The stronger the authority, the stronger the disclosure.

Tool 3: AI Practice Standards

Short norms for teams and vendors. Write the guardrails. Work within them.

Five Standards

  1. Human accountability and appeal every time. A named person is responsible for every automated outcome. People can request human review of any decision.
  2. Plain language disclosure for decision paths. Every system that touches a decision must have a public statement explaining what it does, in language the affected person can understand.
  3. Proportional use matched to risk. The higher the stakes, the more human oversight, the more transparency, and the more robust the appeal process.
  4. Continuous audit and public reporting. Accuracy rates, false positive rates, bias metrics, and override rates published on a regular schedule.
  5. Easy contest and no penalty for asking. Challenging an algorithmic decision must be simple, free, and carry no adverse consequences.

Vendor Terms

All systems supporting public service decisions must meet these requirements. These clauses can be copied directly into RFPs or interagency agreements:

  1. Explainable outputs. Provide plain-language reasons for every automated output.
  2. Human override. Staff must be able to reverse or amend system decisions with documentation.
  3. Full audit trail. Every automated action must log input data, output, and decision logic.
  4. Independent testing. Provide APIs or exports for third-party accuracy and bias review.
  5. Change notice. Any model updates or retraining must be disclosed before deployment.
  6. Accuracy thresholds with remedies. Vendors must publish false positive and false negative rates, with contractual penalties for exceeding limits.
  7. Recourse pathways. End users must have a clear way to request review of automated outcomes.

Worked Examples

Real-world templates for disclosing AI authority across public services. Each example includes the classification level, what the system does, AI's role, human authority, how to challenge decisions, and oversight mechanisms.

Assistive

Medicaid Recertification

Summary

Auto-renews coverage when no changes detected. Flags complex cases for worker review. 60% auto-renewed. 40% need human review. Coverage continues during review.

Service

Medicaid Expansion Eligibility Recertification

Agency

[State] Medicaid Program

What This System Does

Automated recertification every 6 months (federal requirement).

AI's Role

  • Checks databases for income, employment, address changes
  • Auto-renews if no changes and still eligible
  • Flags data mismatches for worker review

Human Authority

  • Workers review all flagged cases
  • Workers determine what additional information is needed
  • Workers make final determination

For Members

  • If auto-renewed: Confirmation notice, no action needed
  • If flagged: Letter explaining what is needed, 30 days to respond

How to Challenge

  • Call worker: [phone]
  • Submit reconsideration if terminated
  • 90-day appeal window

Oversight

  • Monthly monitoring of auto-renewal and termination rates
  • Quarterly review for unnecessary documentation requests
  • Bias testing for disproportionate flagging
Advisory

Unemployment Fraud Detection

Summary

Generates risk scores (0-100). Investigator must find independent evidence before any action. Score cannot deny benefits. Human investigation required. Claimant notified before review begins.

Service

Unemployment Insurance Fraud Detection

Agency

[State] Employment Security Department

What This System Does

Flags claims for potential fraud investigation.

AI's Role (Advisory Only)

  • Generates risk score based on application patterns, data inconsistencies
  • Score alone CANNOT deny benefits or trigger penalties

Human Authority

  • All high-risk scores reviewed by fraud investigator
  • Investigator must document independent evidence
  • No action based on score alone

If Flagged

  • Written notice within 3 business days
  • Right to provide documentation before determination
  • Determination must cite investigator findings, not just score

Oversight

  • Independent audit every 6 months
  • False positive rate published quarterly (target <5%)
  • System disabled if false positive >10%
Safeguards Learned from Michigan MiDAS Failure

Michigan's MiDAS automated fraud detection system produced a 93% false positive rate, wrongly accusing tens of thousands of claimants. The following safeguards are specifically designed to prevent repeating that failure:

  • No automated denials — the system flags, it does not decide
  • Mandatory human review — every flagged case is reviewed by a trained investigator
  • Claimant notification before investigation — people know they have been flagged before any adverse action
Advisory

SNAP Documentation Check

Summary

Scans case files for missing docs. Worker decides if additional info needed. System does NOT determine eligibility, calculate benefits, or auto-deny applications.

Service

SNAP Error Rate Reduction

Agency

[State] Department of Human Services

What This System Does

Helps workers identify missing documentation before federal audits.

AI's Role

  • Scans for missing income verification, address docs, signatures
  • Generates checklist for worker review

Human Authority

  • Worker reviews alerts and decides if additional docs needed
  • Worker can mark alerts "not applicable"
  • Worker makes final eligibility determination

For Applicants

  • What changes: May get requests for additional documentation
  • What doesn't change: Eligibility criteria, benefit amounts, timelines

Why This Exists

Federal law (HR1) penalizes states for insufficient documentation. This helps workers complete case files to protect state SNAP funding.

What This System Does NOT Do

  • Calculate benefit amounts
  • Determine eligibility
  • Auto-deny applications
  • Share data with immigration enforcement
Advisory + Limited Determinative

Child Welfare Hotline Screening

Summary

Generates risk scores (0-20). Scores above 18 trigger mandatory investigation. Below 18, screener decides. "The information summarized by the score does not replace clinical judgment" for 95% of calls.

Service

Child Welfare Hotline Screening

Agency

[County] Department of Human Services

What This System Does

Generates risk assessment scores for incoming hotline calls.

AI's Role

  • Scores 0-20 based on CPS history, child age, allegation type, environmental factors
  • Scores 18-20: Automatic face-to-face investigation within 24 hours (state law)
  • Scores below 18: Score is ONE input; screener decides

Human Authority

  • Screeners review ALL calls
  • Can upgrade response regardless of score
  • Supervisory review required to screen out calls scored above 15

How to Challenge

  • Request supervisor review during call
  • Administrative review within 5 business days
  • Score and notes available through records request

Oversight

  • Weekly screener calibration sessions
  • Quarterly bias audit (race, zip code, reporter type)
  • Annual recalibration
  • Community advisory board reviews aggregate data
Advisory

911 Call Prioritization

Summary

Suggests priority level based on call keywords and location. Dispatcher makes final determination. Dispatcher can override any suggestion. All life-threatening calls get immediate response regardless of system suggestion.

Service

Emergency Call Dispatch

Agency

[City] 911 Communications

What This System Does

Assists dispatchers in prioritizing emergency response.

AI's Role

  • Analyzes call keywords, location, historical patterns
  • Suggests priority level (1-5, 1 being highest)
  • Flags potential life-threatening situations

Human Authority

  • Dispatcher makes all final determinations
  • Can override any suggestion without documentation requirement
  • Life-threatening calls get immediate response regardless of system input

For Callers

System helps speed up response, but trained dispatcher always makes the call about what resources to send.

Oversight

  • Weekly review of override rates
  • Monthly audit of response times by priority level
  • Quarterly review of calls where system suggestion differed significantly from dispatcher decision

System Limitations

  • Cannot assess caller tone or urgency beyond keywords
  • May not recognize regional dialects or uncommon emergency descriptions
  • Dispatcher training emphasizes trusting professional judgment over system

Special Education Screening

Summary

Flags students who may need evaluation. School team makes all decisions about assessment and services. System identifies patterns, does not diagnose or determine eligibility.

Service

Special Education Needs Identification

Agency

[District] Special Education Services

What This System Does

Helps identify students who may benefit from special education evaluation.

AI's Role

  • Analyzes attendance patterns, grades, behavioral referrals
  • Flags students showing patterns associated with learning challenges
  • Does NOT diagnose conditions or determine eligibility

Human Authority

  • Teacher or parent can request evaluation regardless of system flag
  • School team (teachers, specialists, parents) review all flagged cases

For Families

System helps ensure no student is overlooked, but evaluation decisions involve you and the school team, not the algorithm.

  • Parent feedback collected on referral process

What This System Is NOT

  • Not an eligibility determination
  • Cannot be used to deny parent-requested evaluations
Advisory

Building Inspection Prioritization

Summary

Ranks properties for inspection based on risk factors. Inspector decides final inspection schedule and findings. System helps prioritize limited inspection resources. Does not determine pass/fail or penalties.

Service

Building Safety Inspection Scheduling

Agency

[City] Building and Safety Department

What This System Does

Helps prioritize which properties get inspected when.

AI's Role

  • Ranks properties based on: building age, prior violations, complaint history, neighborhood risk factors
  • Suggests inspection priority order
  • Does NOT determine if property passes or fails, or what penalties apply

Human Authority

  • Inspector can move urgent cases up in queue
  • Inspector conducts all inspections and makes findings
  • Inspector determines violations and required corrections

For Property Owners

  • You can request inspection regardless of system priority
  • System ranking does not affect inspection standards or outcomes
  • All violations and penalties determined by licensed inspector

Oversight

  • Monthly review: are high-priority properties actually higher risk?
  • Quarterly demographic analysis to prevent biased targeting
  • Annual review of inspection outcomes vs. system predictions

Transparency

  • Ranking factors publicly documented
  • Property owners can request their property's risk score
  • System logic published publicly

How to Use These Templates

Get Started

  1. Classify your systems using the Decision-Grade AI Taxonomy
  2. Draft Enablement Statements using the template fields
  3. Publish them where people use your service
  4. Share back for others to learn from real implementations

When adapting these templates:

  • Choose the example closest to your use case from the worked examples above
  • Adapt the structure — keep the sections, change the content to match your system
  • Be specific — real numbers, real contact info, real processes
  • Be honest — if accuracy is unknown, say so; if humans rubber-stamp, don't claim review
  • Publish it — put it where people use your service (e.g. /ai or /transparency)
  • Share back — submit your real implementation so others can learn

The credit standard, applied everywhere. We already know disclosure works. FCRA and ECOA require it for credit decisions—even algorithmic ones. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explicitly said: creditors cannot hide behind algorithmic complexity. If an algorithm affects you, you have the right to know.

The core requirements are straightforward: what decision the system makes or influences, what data it uses, where human judgment remains, and how to challenge it when wrong.

Make authority visible. Make decisions contestable. Make systems worthy of trust.

About This Guide

These disclosure standards are designed for real-world deployment under real-world constraints. They are released into the public domain (CC0) so that any agency, vendor, or advocate can use, adapt, and build upon them without licensing concerns.

Every example makes these frameworks more useful. Every improvement makes them more actionable. Real-world experience builds the shared knowledge base that government AI accountability requires.
